on what time reveals

Poetcore and the comfort of old things

← the field notes
crystal st. john may 2026 culture / philosophy

Poetcore is rising in 2026, and what a quiet relief to find that what I've been cultivating for years has a name.

For the unfamiliar: poetcore is a visual aesthetic that romanticizes the lived-in. Old books. Handwritten notes. Candle wax dripping onto wooden tables. Worn cardigans. The clack of an analog typewriter. It longs for beauty, intimacy, and emotional depth in a culture that feels increasingly fast, disposable, and detached.

There's a reason Faeblette aligns with a trend like this. We are all navigating a hyper-modern world, and those of us who feel deeply can't catch our breath. We long for the intimacy that social media has slowly chipped away. We want linen in a world of polyester. Whole foods in a world of ingredients we can't pronounce. Something hand-stitched in place of something factory-made.

It's not that we long for the past. We long for what has integrity, in a world full of illusions. In a world full of content, we want art.

And that's why Faeblette is being cultivated.

✦ ✦ ✦

So why do we love vintage things? Why do interior designers grow elated over the patina on a piece of furniture? What is in the old and the lived-in, really?

As I sat with this question, I started to see the through-line.

Time reveals what something is.

Time reveals the integrity of a material. The resilience of its nature. It diminishes the shine of inexperience. And for people, time humbles us, grounds us in our condition, in our shared humanity. That is what connects us to ourselves, to God, to each other. Empathy, born through the weathering of time.

Children are like new toys. They haven't yet been tested. They haven't yet faced loss. They don't know what they're made of, nor do they know what they lack. With time comes loss, lack, and testing. A woman becomes truly mature when she sees, with equal clarity, both her insignificance in being and her significance in affecting everything within and beyond her sphere.

When I think of old people, I think of books full of hopes and disappointments. Old people have been to many funerals, many of which were for the desires in their own hearts. They've watched impossibly wonderful things happen, but not on their own timetables. They've been woken up with tragedies they wouldn't wish on their neighbors. They've watched the world fall apart and fall back into order again. They know it will happen again.

They've watched people change, and they've watched themselves change. They've watched people stay the same, and they see what is always the same within themselves. They know who they are. Who they've always been. They've chipped away the masks and costumes they once tried on. The pressure of bending to crowds, the lure of jumping on bandwagons headed off cliffs.

They know what is not theirs to carry, so they don't bother trying.

They know they'll be just fine. They've panicked many times before, and the sun still rose. This moment will pass. The good and the bad. The peaks and the valleys, both bringing value.

"Who am I to decide whether I am walking through a peak or a valley? What do I know? I know nothing," says the old person.

There is something humble about old things, and old people. And yet they have the most to be proud of, for they have lived the most.

When we find something that has weathered through time, a person, an object, it inspires us. Maybe we, too, can weather through time. Perhaps we'll come out even more beautiful. More ourselves. Wrinkles and scars, with lessons and stories.

✦ ✦ ✦

I love the old because they seem to have lived long enough to understand, better than I do, what is most important in life.

I love the old because they are slower to judge, for they've watched tragedies turn into answered prayers.

I love the old because they've given up on impressing anyone, yet they remain the most impressive.

And I love the old because they have loved me so.

Here is to the old.

Xx Crystal
